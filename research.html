Many complex systems are governed by interactions that extend beyond pairwise relationships. Across biological, physical, and engineered systems, collective behavior often emerges from interactions involving multiple components simultaneously. My research seeks to understand how these higher-order interactions shape dynamics, how latent interaction structure can be reconstructed from data, and how such systems can be influenced through principled interventions.

My research is organized around three interconnected directions that together form a closed-loop perspective on complex systems: dynamics, inference, and control.

Higher-Order Dynamics

I develop mathematical frameworks for dynamical systems with higher-order interactions with a particular emphasis on pattern formation and instability mechanisms on hypergraphs. My dissertation work focused on a unified theory of structural visibility in higher-order dynamics through pattern forming mechanisms, extending classical reaction–diffusion theory to higher-order interaction structures.

Inference and Reconstruction

I study inverse problems for recovering higher-order interaction structure from dynamical data. This includes questions of identifiability, reconstruction from time-series observations, and the development of computational methods for extracting structurally relevant signatures.

Control

I am interested in how higher-order systems can be influenced, stabilized, or steered through principled interventions. This direction builds on my background in nonlinear control theory and autonomous systems, and aims to develop a mathematical framework for controlling emergent behavior in complex systems.

More broadly, I am interested in extending the mathematical theory of dynamical systems beyond pairwise interactions, and in building frameworks that connect forward modeling, inverse inference, and control.
